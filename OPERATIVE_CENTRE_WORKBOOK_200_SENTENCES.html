1. Purpose, status and boundaries

This document collects the sentence-to-centre-to-coordinate toolbox in one place. It is intended to be usable by a human analyst, a coordinating GPT, or an independent classifier. It integrates the canonical dimensions and cell names with a practical resolution procedure developed through sentence experiments. It does not replace the authoritative controlling reference, and its proposed procedures and example classifications must not be mistaken for additional canonical axioms.

What the method attempts: identify what a complete utterance accomplishes as an intelligible whole; locate the determination responsible for its sufficiency; test the simultaneous form of transformation, completion and persistence; compare a proposed 54-cell identity with genuine rivals.

What it does not guarantee: that every grammatical sentence is a complete unit of intelligibility; that a sentence has one context-independent centre; that every centre has exactly one cell; that 54 is exhaustive; or that agreement among GPTs proves correctness. The 54 is a structured research hypothesis under test.

Source hierarchy: (1) the exact sentence and its declared context; (2) the frozen, independently recorded centre; (3) the fifteen canonical definitions and Dimensional Conjunction rule; (4) neighbour tests and counterfactuals; (5) canonical cell names as checks, not as prompts for reverse engineering. If the evidence conflicts, report the conflict rather than silently forcing a label.

2. Foundational concepts

2.1 Unit of intelligibility (unitus)

A unitus is a sufficiently completed intelligible accomplishment. Its boundary need not coincide perfectly with punctuation or grammar. A full sentence can be underdetermined without context; a short answer or fragment can be complete relative to a live question. For corpus work, treat a sentence as the proposed unit, but test its actual sufficiency.

2.2 Unfolding intelligibility

Read the utterance in order. Record how each meaningful addition changes what is available to understand. This is a diagnostic reconstruction of reading, not a claim that the three dimensions themselves are derived sequentially.

2.3 Operative Centre

Definition used by this toolbox: the minimal governing determination through which an unfolding intelligibility becomes sufficient and holds as achieved.

The centre can be a word, phrase, relation, contrast, condition, causal dependency, ordered sequence, distributed organization, or a whole-sentence configuration. It is not necessarily the grammatical subject, predicate, main clause, conjunction, most vivid image, or the final word.

A useful centre statement has the form: “What becomes sufficient is [determination], through [indispensable dependency/condition, if any].” Do not impose that template when it distorts the sentence.

2.4 Governing location versus centre

The governing location quotes the precise material in the sentence that carries the completion. The Operative Centre states the achieved determination. These may differ: the word because marks an explanation, but the centre may be a functional capacity made intelligible by the organization that produces it.

2.5 Sufficiency and persistence

Ask two separate questions: What finally makes this utterance enough? and in what form is that achieved intelligibility available for uptake? Persistence is about the achieved completion, not simply how long an object lasts or whether a proposition is timeless.

3. The complete canonical vocabulary

The following fifteen definitions are the controlling definitions reproduced from THE_54_CONTROL.md.

3.1 The three primitive dimensions

  1. Transformation Pattern — Specifies what kind of unresolvedness is altered between the opening and completed situation.
  2. Completion Topology — Specifies the structural shape by which the transformation counts as complete.
  3. Persistence Mode — Specifies how the achieved completion remains available for subsequent uptake.

3.2 Six Transformation Patterns

  1. Differentiation — A difference that was unavailable, flattened, conflated, or non-operative becomes determinate enough to matter.
  2. Opening — Availability, reach, continuation, access, or possibility becomes greater than it was under the opening constraint.
  3. Relating — A bearing, dependency, correspondence, ordering, reciprocity, or mutual determination becomes constitutive of the situation.
  4. Repair — An organization that fails relative to an operative criterion is changed so that the misfit or fault no longer governs the result.
  5. Integration — Separate parts acquire determinate roles through organization into a whole and thereby gain structure, mutual support, or joint capacity.
  6. Settlement — A live demand for further uptake, choice, explanation, completion, adjudication, or continuation is discharged.

3.3 Three Completion Topologies

  1. Focal — The transformation becomes complete because completion gathers around one privileged locus. That locus is constitutive of why the transformation counts as complete.
  2. Distributed — The transformation becomes complete only across a constitutive plurality, span, sequence, range, reciprocal field, set of cases, or system. No single locus is sufficient by itself.
  3. Release — The transformation becomes complete because something that previously governed the situation loses its determining force. Release means de-governance, not merely removal, absence, negation, separation, physical movement away, or negative wording.

3.4 Three Persistence Modes

  1. Standing — The achieved determination itself remains available as the completed result.
  2. Bearing — The achieved result remains what it is only through a constitutive relation, dependency, contrast, condition, source, proportion, frame, support, or mutual determination.
  3. Efficacy — The achieved result remains as an accomplished capacity, operative effect, changed continuation, rule, function, or consequential difference in what can happen.

4. Dimensional Conjunction

Dimensional Conjunction is not a fourth dimension. A coordinate is written Transformation Pattern × Completion Topology × Persistence Mode. Its three terms are simultaneous, reciprocally constraining aspects of one completion-event; they are not three detachable properties to add together.

  • The transformation is this transformation completed through this topology in this persistence.
  • The topology is the topology of this transformation in this persistence.
  • The persistence is how this transformation completed through this topology remains achieved.

Non-Cartesian rule: do not semantically derive Transformation → Topology → Persistence as three operations. An analyst may document provisional axis hypotheses in sequence for clarity, but must finally demonstrate one conjunction, and revise the hypotheses if they do not cohere.

Non-operator rule: Focal does not simply select an object; Distributed does not just mean many nouns; Release does not negate the Transformation Pattern; Standing does not simply mean “true”; Bearing does not mean “contains a relationship”; Efficacy does not mean “something useful happens later.”

Relational-field rule: a neighbour differing in one axis is a negative constraint. It should reveal a change in the kind of completion, not a vocabulary substitution.

Unity test: If one can remove the alleged transformation, topology, or persistence and still describe essentially the same completion, the coordinate may be assembled rather than resolved. State the unified characteristic in one natural sentence without mechanically concatenating three definitions.

5. The sentence-resolution procedure

Stage 0 — Freeze the material and conditions

Assign a sentence ID. Preserve the exact wording, punctuation and any declared interpretive context. Note whether the sentence is an empirical claim, conditional law, definition, mathematical theorem, metaphor, command or context-dependent utterance. Do not silently repair ambiguous or scientifically questionable claims. Record the prompt version and whether the analyst has seen the atlas or earlier solutions.

Stage 1 — Sentence-first resolution, before the taxonomy

1. Read the complete sentence without labels. Ask what a competent reader can take as achieved by the end.

2. Reconstruct the unfolding. Mark the initial demand or underdetermination, the successive contributions, and the point at which the utterance becomes sufficient. Distinguish reading sequence from the structural topology of completion.

3. Identify the governing location. Quote the smallest decisive span(s), which may be discontinuous. Say what each contributes and why the whole depends on their configuration.

4. State a minimal Operative Centre. Use one sentence that names the completed determination, not a topic summary. Avoid importing the canonical vocabulary if possible.

5. Test sufficiency. What is no longer left open by this sentence? What exactly would a reader now be entitled to understand, infer or apply? Avoid importing unstated causal mechanisms, values or purposes.

6. Freeze the centre. Save the original wording and rationale with a timestamp or run ID. It must remain recoverable even if later rejected. Do not retroactively edit it to fit a coordinate.

Stage 2 — Canonical classification

7. Read the canonical definitions. Evaluate all six transformation alternatives, all three topologies and all three persistence modes against the same frozen centre.

8. Form one candidate conjunction. Ask how the three proposed constraints determine a single completed intelligibility. A list of independently true properties is insufficient.

9. Name the nearest rivals. Test at least one one-axis neighbour and any serious cross-pattern competitor. Do not automatically treat the numerically adjacent cell as the conceptually nearest rival.

10. Apply counterfactual and deletion tests. Check which part governs the distinctive accomplishment, not merely whether a shortened clause remains grammatical or true.

11. Test the canonical crystallization. Consult the cell name only after deriving the coordinate. It is a crystallization, not a literal checklist; nevertheless, a severe mismatch warrants investigation.

12. Decide and calibrate. Return a preferred coordinate, nearest rival, decisive evidence, confidence, and whether uniqueness is supported or withheld. A preferred candidate and a uniquely established identity are different claims.

Stage 3 — Independent re-examination, if needed

13. Revisit the centre without defending it. Distinguish a governing determination from a merely revealing qualification, and a grammatical relation from a constitutive one.

14. Preserve the audit trail. Report frozen centre → revised centre → original cell → revised cell, with exact reasons. Do not claim the original centre was unchanged if its governing emphasis moved.

15. Stop appropriately. A genuine unresolved neighbour distinction is a valid result. Avoid both forced certainty and performative abstention.

6. The operative-centre diagnostic toolbox

Diagnostic Question What it can show What it cannot prove
Governing-location test Which words or dependency make the full sentence sufficient? Candidate governing span That a conjunction or main clause governs automatically
Prefix/unfolding test What changes as each part arrives? Moment of completion That late words are always the centre
Qualification deletion What distinctive accomplishment disappears if a clause is removed? Constitutive vs supplementary role That the surviving main clause governs
Qualification alteration If the subordinate clause changes while grammar remains, what changes in meaning? Semantic dependency A unique cell by itself
Main-clause alteration Can the qualification remain while the asserted transformation changes? Relative governing weight A mechanical main/subordinate hierarchy
Minimal-pair test What smallest change destroys the proposed centre? Necessary contribution Sufficient evidence of one persistence mode
Contrast reversal What if while, despite, before, equals or because changes its semantic force? Exact role of relation/order/condition That the connective itself is the centre
Causal restraint Does the sentence say after, or does it actually assert because? Limits of causal inference That plausible causation is logically entailed
Criterion test What operative standard is violated, if Repair is proposed? Whether Repair is licensed That every disturbance is a fault
De-governance test What formerly determined the situation, and how does it lose force? Whether Release is licensed That movement, cessation or negation entails Release
Locus test Is one privileged locus constitutive of completion? Focal candidate That a grammatical subject is automatically Focal
Plurality test Could one locus suffice for the same completion? Distributed candidate That two nouns alone establish Distributed
Persistence test Does the achievement stand, depend constitutively, or remain operative? Standing/Bearing/Efficacy discrimination That these modes never overlap in ordinary descriptions
Rival test What does the best alternative explain that the candidate misses? Boundary precision That the analyst's chosen rival is exhaustive
Unity test Are the three axes constraints on one event? Coherence of coordinate Proof of empirical uniqueness

Deletion caution: “An election changes who may decide” remains intelligible without “even when the buildings remain unchanged.” This does not prove that the latter clause was irrelevant; it may disclose the distinction between institutional and physical change. Evaluate the original complete sentence.

Conditional-law caution: A mathematical or scientific condition can be constitutive without every such sentence being Bearing. The question is what the achieved intelligibility principally retains: a standing result, condition-held identity, or operative consequence.

7. The coordinate discrimination toolbox

7.1 Transformation boundaries

  • Differentiation vs Relating: Is the achievement a newly determinate distinction, or a relation/order/dependency that becomes constitutive?
  • Opening vs Relating: Is new possibility/access the achieved point, or is an already present dependency being established?
  • Repair vs Relating: Is there an explicit or defensible fault relative to a criterion, or merely a lawful adjustment to changed conditions?
  • Repair vs Opening: Is an obstruction corrected as a misfit, or does its loss simply enable access?
  • Integration vs Relating: Do parts gain roles as a functioning whole, or is their relation itself sufficient?
  • Settlement vs Repair: Is a live demand discharged, or a criterion-relative fault corrected? Stopping a process need not be either without context.
  • Settlement vs Opening: Does completion discharge a demand, or create new possibility? Both may be described, but which governs?

7.2 Topology boundaries

  • Focal vs Distributed: A focal achievement may involve several objects; a distributed achievement may have a prominent noun. Ask which structure is constitutive of sufficiency.
  • Release vs Focal/Distributed: Release is not a count or location. Identify the previous governor and its loss of determining force. A release may happen across a system, but the topology is Release only if de-governance is why completion counts.

7.3 Persistence boundaries

  • Standing vs Bearing: Is the achieved determination available as established, or does its identity remain constituted through a contrast, source, proportion or condition? Do not infer Bearing merely because the sentence contains “while,” “because,” or “depends.”
  • Bearing vs Efficacy: Is the achievement principally a condition-held relation, or an operative capacity/change in what can happen? A causal explanation may support both; locate what the sentence completes.
  • Standing vs Efficacy: Is the result a standing determination or a consequential rule/capacity? A theorem's timeless truth is not sufficient to classify every scientific law as Standing.

7.4 One-axis neighbour discipline

Hold two axes fixed, change the third, and ask what distinct completion would result. Example: Integration × Distributed × Bearing (41) emphasizes the constitutive mutual-support structure; Integration × Distributed × Efficacy (42) emphasizes the joint operative capacity achieved through it. The sentence “The shelf holds weight because its parts brace one another” has been proposed as 42, with 41 as its nearest rival.

7.5 Evidence hierarchy for a candidate

  1. Exact sentence and declared context.
  2. Frozen centre and sufficiency argument.
  3. Canonical definitions applied as one conjunction.
  4. Specific counterfactual and rival discrimination.
  5. Canonical crystallization fit.
  6. Analyst confidence and external agreement (useful, but never substitutes for the above).

9. Worked resolutions and unresolved boundaries

These are proposed analyses, not externally established answers. Preserve disagreements as evidence.

Example A — Supporting structure

Sentence: “The shelf holds weight because its parts brace one another.”

Governing location: “holds weight because its parts brace one another.”

Centre: The shelf's load-bearing capacity is achieved through the mutual support of its parts.

Preferred: 42 — Integration × Distributed × Efficacy (Role activation).

Rival: 41 — Integration × Distributed × Bearing, if the completed achievement is the mutual-support structure rather than the load-bearing function.

Test: “The parts brace one another, but the shelf cannot hold weight” removes the achieved joint capacity. The test supports 42 but does not logically prove uniqueness.

Example B — Obstruction loses force

Sentence: “The bicycle can pass now that the last box has moved.”

Centre: Passage becomes possible because the final obstruction no longer prevents it.

Preferred: 18 — Opening × Release × Efficacy (Edge opening).

Rival: 36 — Repair × Release × Efficacy, if the obstruction is explicitly a fault against a functional standard.

Critical test: The movement of the box alone is not Release; its loss of determining force over passage is.

Example C — Contrast constitutes prominence

Sentence: “The red square stands out against the grey ones.”

Centre: A particular square's prominence is constituted by its contrast with the grey comparison field.

Preferred: 02 — Differentiation × Focal × Bearing (Binary bearing).

Rival: 05 — Differentiation × Distributed × Bearing, if a field of context-sensitive distinctions rather than one salient square governs.

Example D — A genuine topology disagreement

Sentence: “The machine stopped rattling after the loose bolt was tightened.”

Centre: Rattling ceases following correction of a loose component; causation is suggested, not entailed by after.

Previously proposed reference: 36 — Repair × Release × Efficacy.

Independent GPT candidate: 30 — Repair × Focal × Efficacy.

Open issue: Does completion principally depend on the fault losing governing force (Release), or on a privileged local repair (Focal)? The independent GPT chose 30; this is a disagreement, not an automatically wrong answer. Neither reading should assert that “after” proves causation.

Example E — Explicit relational cycle

Sentence: “Each adjustment changes the next, and the last changes the first.”

Centre: Changes propagate through a sequence and return to its beginning.

Preferred: 24 — Relating × Distributed × Efficacy (Relational cycle).

Rival: 23 — Relating × Distributed × Bearing, if mutual dependency, rather than operative propagation, governs.

Test: Remove “and the last changes the first”; a chain remains, but its completed circuit disappears.

Example F — Temporal priority

Sentence: “A newborn recognizes rhythms before learning the names of the people nearby.”

Centre: Rhythmic recognition is already available before the acquisition of names.

Preferred: 22 — Relating × Distributed × Standing (Sequential link).

Caution: The “prior availability” reading could invite Opening; the governing claim must be tested as temporal ordering, not selected merely because the word before appears.

Example G — Institutional rather than physical change

Sentence: “An election changes who may decide, even when the buildings remain unchanged.”

Frozen centre: Authority can change despite physical sameness.

Revised centre: An election effects a change in decision-making authority, independent of physical alteration of buildings.

Original candidate: 23 — Relating × Distributed × Bearing.

Revised preferred candidate: 03 — Differentiation × Focal × Efficacy (Boundary act); uniqueness withheld.

Key lesson: A centre revision can alter all three axes. The surviving main clause does not automatically prove priority, but the substantive authority change may govern more than the concessive connective.

Example H — Pythagoras' theorem

Sentence: “In a right-angled triangle, the square of the length of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the lengths of the other two sides.”

Centre: The squared hypotenuse is exactly determined by the sum of the squared legs under the right-angle condition.

Preferred: 23 — Relating × Distributed × Bearing.

Rival: 22 — Relating × Distributed × Standing.

Boundary: A condition-held quantitative dependency versus a standing established equality. Two analyses agreed on 23, but agreement does not settle this distinction.

Example I — Le Chatelier's principle

Sentence: “When a system at chemical equilibrium is disturbed by a change in concentration, pressure, or temperature, its equilibrium composition shifts in the direction that tends to oppose the imposed disturbance.”

Centre: A disturbance induces a condition-governed, counteracting equilibrium adjustment.

One analysis: 33 — Repair × Distributed × Efficacy.

Independent analysis: 32 — Repair × Distributed × Bearing.

Cross-pattern rival: 24 — Relating × Distributed × Efficacy, or 23 if Bearing governs.

Boundaries: (a) a physical disturbance is not automatically a criterion-relative fault; (b) a condition-dependent response may be described as both Bearing and Efficacy. The original equilibrium is not necessarily restored; a new equilibrium may result. This case should remain unresolved, not be used as a positive unique-cell demonstration.

10. Protocols for independent tests

10.1 Blind centre-first protocol (A → B)

Stage A — Centre assay: Give the analyst the exact sentences, a neutral instruction to identify their governing determinations, and no 54 lookup, examples, prior answers or cell names. Require governing location, unfolding, sufficiency and frozen centre. Save unedited outputs.

Stage B — Coordinate decoding: Supply the canonical definitions and conjunction rule after centres are frozen. Require a cell, strongest rival, counterfactual, confidence and explicit statement of whether the frozen centre changed. Do not rewrite Stage A as if Stage B's insights were already known.

10.2 Coordinate-first derivation protocol (D)

This is a different instrument. GPT-D receives a coordinate and the frozen derivation prompt, not a sentence or the 54 lookup. It derives a unified form and companion utterance. The full frozen GPT-D prompt is maintained verbatim in THE_54_CONTROL.md; do not paraphrase this toolbox as a substitute for that controlled prompt. The Coordinator alone compares the frozen derivation with the canonical crystallization.

10.3 Round trip

Sentence → frozen centre → proposed coordinate → blind coordinate-derived form → structural comparison. Keep the sentence classifier and coordinate derivation instrument informationally separate. Agreement should be assessed at the level of governing structure, not merely shared trigger words or canonical names.

10.4 Corpus integrity

For a science corpus or a 100-sentence general corpus: preserve exact text and IDs; document how sentences were sampled; do not rebalance them to make all 54 cells appear; avoid giving the independent GPT previous solutions; freeze all outputs before feedback; and distinguish constructed examples from genuinely unseen cases.

10.5 Disagreement adjudication

When two analysts disagree: (1) compare their frozen centres; (2) if centres differ, resolve the governing determination first; (3) if centres match, isolate the first differing axis; (4) ask each analyst for the best rival and a counterfactual discriminant; (5) keep a recorded unresolved category if the definitions do not decide. Never settle disagreement by majority vote alone.

11. Scoring, uncertainty and reporting

11.1 Score components separately

  • Centre agreement: exact wording is not required; compare governing determination, dependency and sufficiency condition. Code as substantive agreement, partial overlap, or disagreement, with justification.
  • Axis agreement: count exact matches across the three canonical dimensions, e.g. 14/15.
  • Exact-cell agreement: count exact coordinate matches, e.g. 4/5.
  • Uniqueness: separate preferred coordinate from unique identity established.
  • Calibration: record confidence, withheld cases, and whether later re-examination changed the centre or coordinate.

Never treat reference solutions as infallible ground truth. A constructed laboratory key measures agreement with an earlier interpretation, not classification accuracy in the scientific sense.

11.2 Suggested confidence language (procedural, not canonical)

  • High: one conjunction strongly discriminates the strongest plausible rivals under the exact wording.
  • Medium-high: a preferred conjunction is well supported, with a limited unresolved neighbour.
  • Medium: meaningful competition remains, though one candidate is preferred.
  • Low / uniqueness withheld: evidence does not reliably discriminate alternatives.
  • Unclassifiable as given: context or sufficiency is too incomplete to identify a governing centre.

Confidence is not a statistical probability unless calibrated against a validated dataset.

13. Failure modes and quality-control checklist

13.1 Common failure modes

  1. Keyword matching: “before” automatically means Relating, “no longer” means Release, “because” means Bearing, or “can” means Efficacy.
  2. Main-clause privilege: a clause is declared governing because it survives deletion.
  3. Connective privilege: a concessive or temporal link is treated as the centre without testing the substantive completion.
  4. Crystallization literalism: “Relational cycle” is required to be a literal feedback loop in every Cell 24 instance.
  5. Additive classification: three compatible properties are listed without one unified completion.
  6. False Repair: every adjustment, response or opposition is called fault correction without a criterion.
  7. False Release: mere absence, negation, movement or stopping is called de-governance.
  8. Object/process confusion: a persisting tree or enduring theorem is mistaken for the persistence mode of intelligibility.
  9. Causal inflation: after is silently converted into because.
  10. Retrospective laundering: a revised centre is presented as though it had been frozen originally.
  11. False certainty: a plausible coordinate is reported as uniquely proven without defeating serious rivals.
  12. False caution: uniqueness is withheld simply to sound rigorous, even when a discriminant is decisive.
  13. Contamination: a blind classifier sees canonical examples or reference answers before producing its frozen response.
  14. Agreement inflation: a 4/5 match on constructed examples is reported as 80% validated real-world accuracy.

13.2 Final quality-control questions

  • Is the sentence reproduced exactly and its context declared?
  • Does the centre identify an achieved determination rather than summarize the topic?
  • Are the decisive governing words/dependencies quoted?
  • Has the sentence's distinctive accomplishment survived scrutiny of deletion and alteration?
  • Have the three axes been tested on one completion, rather than on separate clauses?
  • Is any proposed Repair grounded in a criterion of fault?
  • Is any proposed Release grounded in an identifiable former governor losing force?
  • Is Distributed justified by constitutive plurality, not merely plural grammar?
  • Does Persistence concern the completed intelligibility, not merely a physical object or an external effect?
  • Has the strongest plausible neighbour been genuinely distinguished?
  • Is the crystallization used as a check rather than a literal template?
  • Are frozen and revised analyses kept distinct?
  • Is uncertainty stated at the exact unresolved boundary?
  • Are independent runs truly independent of withheld answers?

Science: 100 Sentences for Independent Operative Centre Testing

This corpus samples established scientific laws, relationships, processes, mechanisms, and findings across ten disciplines. Sentences were chosen for clarity, independent intelligibility, and scientifically appropriate qualifications rather than for any anticipated philosophical classification. The corpus contains no prior 54-cell classifications.

1. Physics

  1. An object moving at constant velocity continues in a straight line unless a net external force acts on it.
  2. The acceleration of an object equals the net force on it divided by its mass when the mass is constant.
  3. For every force one object exerts on another, the second object exerts an equal and opposite force on the first.
  4. The gravitational attraction between two point masses decreases with the square of the distance between them.
  5. In an isolated system, total energy remains constant even when energy changes form.
  6. Light travels more slowly through ordinary glass than through a vacuum.
  7. A sound wave travels through air by producing successive regions of compression and rarefaction.
  8. Increasing the temperature of a metal rod generally causes it to expand.
  9. A moving electric charge experiences a magnetic force when its motion has a component perpendicular to a magnetic field.
  10. The pressure of a gas increases when its volume decreases at constant temperature and fixed gas quantity.

2. Chemistry

  1. At chemical equilibrium, the forward and reverse reactions occur at equal rates.
  2. Adding a reactant to a system at equilibrium can shift its composition toward products when other conditions remain fixed.
  3. An acid donates a proton to a base in a Brønsted–Lowry acid–base reaction.
  4. Water molecules form hydrogen bonds with nearby water molecules.
  5. Sodium chloride dissolves in water as sodium and chloride ions become surrounded by water molecules.
  6. A catalyst speeds a reaction by providing a pathway with a lower activation energy.
  7. The pH of an aqueous solution decreases as its hydrogen ion activity increases.
  8. Carbon atoms can form stable chains because they make strong covalent bonds with other carbon atoms.
  9. An oxidation reaction involves the loss of electrons by a chemical species.
  10. At constant pressure, a pure substance boils when its vapor pressure equals the surrounding pressure.

3. Biology

  1. Cells use ribosomes to assemble proteins from amino acids according to messenger RNA sequences.
  2. During DNA replication, each new DNA double helix normally contains one original strand and one newly synthesized strand.
  3. Natural selection can increase the frequency of inherited traits that improve reproductive success in a particular environment.
  4. Most plants use light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into sugars during photosynthesis.
  5. Enzymes accelerate biochemical reactions without being permanently consumed by those reactions.
  6. A cell membrane regulates the movement of substances between the cell and its surroundings.
  7. Meiosis produces cells with half the chromosome number of the original diploid cell in typical sexual life cycles.
  8. Mutations introduce changes into DNA sequences that can be passed to daughter cells.
  9. Many bacteria reproduce by dividing one cell into two daughter cells.
  10. In multicellular organisms, specialized cells perform different functions through differences in gene expression.

4. Astronomy and Cosmology

  1. Earth orbits the Sun approximately once every year.
  2. The Moon's changing phases result from the changing portion of its sunlit half visible from Earth.
  3. Stars produce much of their energy by fusing lighter atomic nuclei into heavier ones.
  4. The Sun's gravity keeps the planets in bound orbits around it.
  5. Light from distant galaxies is generally redshifted as the universe expands.
  6. A black hole has an event horizon beyond which light cannot escape to distant observers.
  7. The cosmic microwave background is radiation left over from an early hot phase of the universe.
  8. A planet transiting its star can cause a small, periodic decrease in the star's observed brightness.
  9. The seasons on Earth arise mainly from the tilt of Earth's rotation axis relative to its orbit.
  10. A star's apparent brightness depends on both its luminosity and its distance from the observer.

5. Earth Science and Geology

  1. Tectonic plates move slowly relative to one another across Earth's surface.
  2. Earthquakes occur when stored elastic strain is suddenly released along a fault.
  3. Igneous rock forms when molten rock cools and solidifies.
  4. Sedimentary layers often preserve a record of the environments in which their sediments accumulated.
  5. Water expands as it freezes, allowing ice to widen cracks in rocks.
  6. Volcanoes can form where one tectonic plate descends beneath another.
  7. Earth's magnetic field is generated mainly by motion of electrically conducting liquid iron in its outer core.
  8. Rivers transport sediment downstream when flowing water exerts enough force to move particles.
  9. Metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks change under heat, pressure, or chemically active fluids without completely melting.
  10. The age of some rocks can be estimated from the measured proportions of radioactive parent isotopes and their decay products.

6. Ecology and Environmental Science

  1. Plants capture solar energy that can pass through food webs when organisms eat one another.
  2. Decomposers return nutrients from dead organisms to soil and water.
  3. A predator population can decline when its principal prey becomes scarce.
  4. Greater atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations increase Earth's greenhouse effect when other relevant factors are held constant.
  5. Excess nitrogen and phosphorus entering lakes can stimulate algal blooms.
  6. Habitat fragmentation can isolate wildlife populations and reduce movement between them.
  7. Pollinators transfer pollen between flowers and enable reproduction in many flowering plants.
  8. An invasive species can reduce native species populations by competing for limited resources.
  9. Wetlands can reduce downstream flooding by storing water during heavy rainfall.
  10. Ocean acidification occurs as seawater absorbs additional atmospheric carbon dioxide and its pH decreases.

7. Mathematics and Statistics

  1. In a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse's length equals the sum of the squares of the other two side lengths.
  2. The angles inside any triangle on a flat Euclidean plane add up to 180 degrees.
  3. Multiplying a nonzero number by its reciprocal gives one.
  4. The derivative of a differentiable function gives its instantaneous rate of change.
  5. The definite integral of a nonnegative continuous function over an interval equals the area beneath its graph on that interval.
  6. The mean of a finite set of numbers equals their sum divided by the number of values.
  7. The median of an ordered numerical dataset separates its lower and upper halves.
  8. Independent events have a joint probability equal to the product of their individual probabilities.
  9. A larger random sample generally reduces the sampling variability of a sample mean when observations are independent and have finite variance.
  10. A correlation between two variables does not by itself establish that either variable causes the other.

8. Neuroscience and Cognitive Science

  1. Neurons communicate across many synapses by releasing chemical neurotransmitters.
  2. A neuron can generate an action potential when changes in its membrane voltage reach a sufficient threshold.
  3. Myelin increases the speed of electrical signal conduction along many nerve fibers.
  4. The hippocampus is important for forming many new episodic memories.
  5. Visual signals from the retina travel toward the brain through the optic nerve.
  6. Repeated practice can improve performance on a motor skill by changing neural processing.
  7. Attention can improve the processing of selected information while other information receives less processing.
  8. Sleep supports the consolidation of some forms of memory.
  9. The brain combines information from multiple senses to estimate properties of the environment.
  10. Damage to particular brain regions can impair specific cognitive functions while leaving others relatively intact.

9. Medicine and Human Physiology

  1. The heart pumps blood through the lungs and the rest of the body.
  2. Insulin helps many body cells take up glucose from the blood.
  3. The kidneys filter blood and regulate the excretion of water and dissolved substances.
  4. Vaccination can prepare the immune system to respond more effectively to a later infection by a targeted pathogen.
  5. Hemoglobin in red blood cells carries most of the oxygen transported in human blood.
  6. During inhalation, contraction of the diaphragm helps expand the chest cavity and draw air into the lungs.
  7. Fever can occur when immune signals raise the body's temperature set point during infection.
  8. Antibiotics that target bacteria do not directly kill viruses.
  9. The liver converts ammonia produced during amino acid metabolism into urea for excretion.
  10. Regular aerobic exercise can improve the heart's ability to deliver oxygenated blood during physical activity.

10. Computer Science and Information Theory

  1. A binary digit represents one of two possible values.
  2. A computer program follows instructions that specify operations on data.
  3. A sorting algorithm arranges items according to a specified ordering rule.
  4. Binary search can locate a target in a sorted array by repeatedly eliminating half of the remaining search interval.
  5. Encryption transforms readable information into a form that requires an appropriate key to recover the original information.
  6. A checksum can detect some accidental changes to transmitted data.
  7. Lossless compression represents data with fewer bits while allowing exact reconstruction of the original data.
  8. A database index can speed up searches by providing a structure for locating records without scanning every record.
  9. A network packet can reach its destination through a sequence of routers that forward it toward the destination address.
  10. Shannon entropy measures the average uncertainty of a random variable using the probabilities of its possible outcomes.
