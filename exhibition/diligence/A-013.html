Gate One — source analysis

The first question in a classroom can change what everyone notices afterward.

Unfolding and competing Centres

The first classroom question identifies a particular initiating contribution. “Can change” attributes potential influence without asserting that change always or already occurs. The final contribution specifies the influence as subsequent noticing across everyone in the classroom.

The owner proposed Cell 19 before the investigation and requested that the resolution proceed independently of that proposal. The reasoning tests what the completed utterance establishes and preserves the later comparison with that proposed cell.

  1. The first classroom question precedes later noticing. Too weak: succession omits possible influence.
  2. The first classroom question changes everyone's subsequent attention. Too strong: turns possibility into actual occurrence.
  3. The first classroom question can redirect what everyone notices subsequently. Strongest: preserves source, potential influence, scope and direction.

Verification tests

TestFinding
DeletionRemove later noticing and the question's particular influence is unspecified.
AlterationReplace “can change” with “precedes”: succession remains but influence disappears.
NarrowingReplace “everyone” with “one pupil”: the scope of potential influence narrows. Changing “first” to “a” loses the initiating position without showing that later questions lack influence.
ReversalMake later noticing change the first question: direction reverses.
DependencySubsequent noticing is susceptible to influence from the question. No exclusive control or particular mechanism is established.
Sufficiency-locationInfluence gathers around the identified initiating question, with its reach specified through later noticing.
Minimal invalidationReplace “can change” with “cannot affect”: the participants and temporal setting remain, but the attributed potential disappears.

Six confirmation questions

Four holding tests

  1. Identity: an attribution of potential influence, not merely a connection or an event already performed.
  2. Constitutive dependencies: initiating question, subsequent noticing and direction identify this potential. Without influence, only temporal succession remains. Other influences are not excluded.
  3. Operative continuation: yes; a possible difference to what happens afterward is expressly attributed. This is not an imagined practical application, and actual exercise is not asserted.
  4. Minimal invalidation: “cannot affect” defeats the potential while retaining the question and subsequent noticing.

Achieved-identity discrimination

What has been established is: the first classroom question's potential to redirect everyone's subsequent noticing.

What obtains through what is: a possible change in subsequent noticing through the influence of that initiating question.

Both identify the same potential influence. Neither licenses turning possibility into guaranteed or already accomplished redirection.
